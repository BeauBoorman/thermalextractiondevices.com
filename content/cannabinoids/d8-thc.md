# Δ8-Tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ8-THC)

## Identity

| Property | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Preferred name | Δ8-Tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ8-THC) |
| IUPAC name | (6aR,10aR)-6,6,9-trimethyl-3-pentyl-6a,7,10,10a-tetrahydrobenzo[c]chromen-1-ol |
| CAS number | 5957-75-5 |
| PubChem CID | 638026 |
| InChIKey | HCAWPGARWVBULJ-IAGOWNOFSA-N [^1] |
| Molecular formula | C21H30O2 |
| Molecular mass | 314.46 g/mol (314.5) |
| Compound class | Phytocannabinoid; dibenzopyran cannabinoid; positional double-bond isomer of Δ9-THC |
| Stereochemistry | Two stereocenters; natural material is (6aR,10aR) trans; distinct identity from Δ9-THC [^1] |
| Major synonyms | Δ8-THC, delta-8-THC, Δ8-trans-tetrahydrocannabinol; dronabinol-class isomer (dronabinol itself is Δ9) |

### Identity notes

- Δ8-THC and Δ9-THC differ only in double-bond position (8(9) vs. 9(10)) but are distinct compounds with distinct CAS numbers (5957-75-5 vs. 1972-08-3) and distinct legal treatment in many jurisdictions; never collapse the two [^1].
- Δ8-THC occurs naturally in trace amounts and is also produced by acid-catalyzed isomerization of CBD during commercial concentrate processing; analytical distinction from Δ9-THC and from Δ9,11/Δ4(8)-iso-THC artifacts requires chromatographic resolution [^2].

## Physical properties

| Property | Value | Conditions / Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Boiling point | **No compound-specific measured atmospheric boiling point was located.** A correlation-GC vapor-pressure evaluation of Δ8-THC exists (Chickos 2026), but decomposition prevents a directly observed atmospheric boil | Prediction/correlation, not an observation [^3][^4] |
| Vapor pressure | Evaluated by correlation gas chromatography against a surrogate and compared with experimental Δ9-THC data; no standalone directly measured Δ8-THC curve was located | Correlation, not direct measurement [^3] |
| Melting point | Not a sharp solid; the isolated compound is a viscous resin/oil (literature values refer to specific polymorphs/solvates and are not reproduced here) | Unresolved |
| logP (octanol-water) | ≈6.0–6.5 (predicted) | Highly lipophilic |
| Water solubility | Practically insoluble | Soluble in ethanol, lipids |
| Thermal decomposition | Δ8-THC is formed as a thermal/isomerization product of CBD in hot GC injectors (with Δ9-THC) and accumulates in aged/processed material; a Δ8-THC-specific decomposition onset was not located | Study conditions [^2][^5] |
| Oxidation behavior | Expected to oxidize toward CBN by analogy to Δ9-THC; compound-specific rates are uncharacterized | Analogy, not measurement [^5] |
| Known degradation products | Oxidation toward CBN by analogy to Δ9-THC; additional isomerization products under strongly acidic/thermal processing remain uncharacterized | [^5] |

> **Boiling point is not a device setpoint.** No measured atmospheric boiling point exists for Δ8-THC. Marketing figures ("Δ8 boils at 175–178 °C") are not thermodynamic properties; the correlation-GC evaluation is a derived estimate, and device behavior must come from per-device testing [^3][^4].

## Thermal-extraction context

{{include includes/boiling-point-vs-device-note.md}}

Δ8-THC volatilizes from matrix by vapor-pressure-driven evaporation; its volatility is expected to track Δ9-THC closely (same formula, same mass, near-identical vaporization enthalpy by correlation), but compound-specific device-release data are unresolved [^3]. Device chamber temperature is not sample temperature; the thermodynamic boiling point of the pure compound is not a device setpoint or a sample temperature.

## Cannabis occurrence

Trace natural occurrence in cannabis; most commercial Δ8 material is manufactured by CBD isomerization rather than extracted. Occurrence claims on consumer products are process claims, not botanical measurements, and are not treated as batch evidence here [^2].

## Biosynthesis and processing

Δ8-THC is not a primary biosynthetic product: it forms by isomerization of Δ9-THC or acid-catalyzed cyclization of CBD during processing, storage, and hot-injector analysis [^2][^5]. Thermal treatments convert CBD partially to Δ8-THC alongside Δ9-THC under the cited conditions [^5].

## Reported biological activity

### Human clinical evidence
Controlled human comparisons exist from 1973: oral and intravenous Δ8-THC produced Δ9-like psychological and physiological effects with roughly two-thirds to similar potency by the studied routes [^6]. An open-label 1995 pediatric oncology study reported antiemetic effect at 18 mg/m² oral Δ8-THC [^7]. No modern randomized trial of Δ8 products was located; these older studies used pharmaceutical-grade material, not current hemp-derived commercial products [^8].

### Human observational evidence
Survey studies of Δ8 consumers report self-described therapeutic substitution and Δ9-like intoxicating effects; surveys are not clinical evidence [^9].

### Preclinical animal and in vitro evidence

{{include includes/preclinical-evidence-note.md}}

Cannabinoid receptor activity of Δ8-THC is well characterized (CB1/CB2 agonist, somewhat lower potency than Δ9-THC) [^10].

### Industry claims
"Δ8 is legal everywhere," "Δ8 is a mild high," and "Δ8 is natural" are marketing claims; legal status varies by jurisdiction and most commercial Δ8 is synthesized from CBD [^8][^9].

## Degradation products

- Oxidation: toward CBN by analogy to Δ9-THC (uncharacterized rates) [^5]
- Isomerization products under strong acid/heat: uncharacterized
