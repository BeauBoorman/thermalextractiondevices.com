# Cannabinol (CBN)

## Identity

| Property | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Preferred name | Cannabinol (CBN) |
| IUPAC name | 6,6,9-trimethyl-3-pentylbenzo[c]chromen-1-ol |
| CAS number | 521-35-7 |
| PubChem CID | 2543 |
| InChIKey | VBGLYOIFKLUMQG-UHFFFAOYSA-N [^1] |
| Molecular formula | C21H26O2 |
| Molecular mass | 310.44 g/mol (310.4) |
| Compound class | Phytocannabinoid; tricyclic aromatic cannabinoid (fully aromatized dibenzopyran) |
| Stereochemistry | Fully aromatic C-ring; no stereocenters in the canonical structure [^1] |
| Major synonyms | Cannabinol, CBN; not to be confused with the acid CBNA or the propyl homologue CBN-C3 |

### Identity notes

- CBN is the fully aromatized oxidation product of Δ9-THC; it is a distinct compound, not a "aged THC" label [^1].
- Structure established by synthesis in 1940 (Adams et al. [^2]; Ghosh, Todd and Wilkinson [^3]).

## Physical properties

| Property | Value | Conditions / Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Melting point | ≈76–77 °C (crystalline solid; triple point 352.15 K reported in the cited vapor-pressure study) | Pure compound [^4] |
| Boiling point | **No observed atmospheric boiling point.** Thermal decomposition precedes boiling at 1 atm; no experimental normal boiling temperature was located | Predictions depend on vapor-pressure extrapolation [^4][^5] |
| Vapor pressure | Measured 0.0199–5.678 Pa over 364–424 K (91–151 °C) by dynamic vapor microextraction (gas saturation); correlation-derived p ≈ 2.3×10⁻⁵ Pa at 307.15 K | Direct measurement; uncertainty ≈3–5 % of p_sat [^4] |
| logP (octanol-water) | ≈6.2–6.5 (predicted) | Highly lipophilic |
| Water solubility | Practically insoluble (<1 µg/mL estimated) | Soluble in ethanol, DMSO, lipids |
| Thermal decomposition | No compound-specific decomposition onset was located. In the cited GC-injector/thermal-treatment study CBN was a terminal oxidation product that accumulated while THC degraded; CBN loss at higher temperatures is condition-dependent and not assigned a universal onset | Injector/residence conditions are study conditions [^6] |
| Oxidation behavior | CBN is itself the oxidation product of THC and is comparatively stable; further oxidative chemistry under device conditions remains uncharacterized | Condition-dependent [^6] |
| Known degradation products | None characterized under cited conditions; CBN accumulates as the degradation product of other cannabinoids | [^6] |

> **Boiling point is not a device setpoint.** CBN is one of the few cannabinoids with a directly measured vapor-pressure curve (364–424 K) [^4], and those data show no observed atmospheric boiling point; marketing figures such as "CBN boils at 185 °C" are device-setting folklore, not thermodynamic properties [^5].

## Thermal-extraction context

{{include includes/boiling-point-vs-device-note.md}}

CBN volatilizes from botanical matrix by vapor-pressure-driven evaporation with no decarboxylation step required (the neutral compound is already oxidized). Release depends on load, airflow, and residence time; at common device operating temperatures the measured curve (to 151 °C) is close to its upper end, and behavior above that is extrapolation, not measurement [^4]. Device chamber temperature is not sample temperature; the thermodynamic boiling point of the pure compound is not a device setpoint or a sample temperature.

## Cannabis occurrence

CBN is a minor fresh-flower constituent that increases with storage age and oxidation; measured batches in legal-market datasets report wide batch-to-batch CBN ranges that track product age and storage rather than cultivar identity [^7]. Values are batch- and report-attached; no universal cultivar claim is made. Consult [Lab Results](../lab-results.md) for batch-level measurements.

## Biosynthesis and processing

CBN is not biosynthesized directly in quantity: it forms abiotically by oxidation of Δ9-THC (itself the decarboxylation product of THCA) during storage, drying, and heating [^6]. The acid form CBNA decarboxylates to CBN on heating [^8].

## Reported biological activity

### Human clinical evidence
No randomized controlled trial of isolated CBN for any indication was identified in this archive's literature review (as of 2026-08-28). A peer-reviewed review of the "CBN for sleep" claim found no controlled human evidence supporting CBN as a sedative at consumer-relevant doses and traced the claim to older studies of crude extracts and misread mixture data [^9].

### Human observational evidence
None identified for isolated CBN.

### Preclinical animal and in vitro evidence

{{include includes/preclinical-evidence-note.md}}

In vitro studies report CBN affinity for CB1 and CB2 receptors in the low-to-mid micromolar range — materially weaker than Δ9-THC — and CB2-mediated effects in cell systems [^10][^11]. In vitro screens report antitumor activity against acute myeloid leukemia cell lines at micromolar concentrations [^12]. El-Alfy et al. found no antidepressant-like effect of CBN in the mouse forced-swim test at screened doses [^13]. None of this establishes effects in people.

### Industry claims
"CBN = the sleep cannabinoid" is a marketing claim without controlled human evidence for isolated CBN [^9]; "CBN is non-psychoactive" overstates the evidence — CBN is far less potent than Δ9-THC but is not established as inactive [^10].

## Degradation products

- None characterized under the cited conditions; CBN is a terminal oxidation product and accumulates as other cannabinoids degrade [^6]
